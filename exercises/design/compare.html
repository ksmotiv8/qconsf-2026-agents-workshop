I went to QCon SF (https://qconsf.com) to teach people how to make an AI sound less like an AI. Ruth Linehan and I co-ran a hands-on workshop on Claude Code and Codex. Thank you, Ruth. Co-teaching with you made the whole thing better. The exercise had two steps. Step one: everyone used an agent to write a LinkedIn post. No special setup, no tricks. Just ask and take what comes back. Step two: everyone built a skill that writes in their own voice, then used it to write the post again. What happened in between was my favorite part of the week. People read that first draft. Then they winced. You could see it happen across the room. Every one of us knows that tone: the breathless opener, the tidy list of three, the confident paragraph that says nothing. Generic text is a rental car. It gets you there, and nobody would mistake it for yours. Then came the good bit. Nobody asked me how to fix it. They fixed it themselves. They knew exactly what was wrong because they know how they sound, and the skill was how they taught that to the agent. That's the lesson I'd take from the room. The agent can type faster than you. Only you can tell it what "you" sounds like. Want to try it? The whole exercise is in our repo, and you can run it on your own time, with your own taste as the test: https://github.com/ksmotiv8/qconsf-2026-agents-workshop If you try it, I'd love to hear how your first draft compared to your second. Bonus points for the wince. #QConSF #ClaudeCode #Codex #AIAgents

I went to QCon SF (https://qconsf.com) and made a room full of engineers ask an AI to write a LinkedIn post. Then I made them read it out loud. In their heads, thankfully. Ruth Linehan and I co-ran a hands-on workshop on Claude Code and Codex, and the exercise had two parts. **Part one: write the post with an agent.** Everyone got a first draft in seconds. Fast, fluent, and about as personal as a hotel lobby. **Part two: build a skill that writes in your own voice.** Same agent, same topic, but now it has been taught how *you* actually write. The best moment of the whole conference came between the two. People read that first draft, and you could see it happen. The wince. The slow head shake. Someone whispering "I would never say that." Nobody had to tell them it was slop. They spotted it themselves, and then they fixed it themselves. That's the part I care about. An agent that can write is table stakes. An agent that writes like you, because you took the time to tell it how, is the one you'll trust with your name on the post. The fix wasn't a smarter model. It was a person who knew what good sounded like. Big thanks to Ruth for co-running this with me, and to everyone who showed up, typed, winced, and rewrote. Want to try it? The whole exercise is in our repo: https://github.com/ksmotiv8/qconsf-2026-agents-workshop Run it, then tell me how your first draft read. I'd love to hear how much slop you caught.
